# Research Methodology

## Design and philosophy

The study used an **interpretivist** research philosophy and an **inductive qualitative** approach. Its interest was not only which metrics managers viewed, but how they interpreted information, reconciled disagreement between analytics and experience, and acted under uncertainty.

## Sampling and participants

- **Sampling:** Purposive, selecting participants with relevant managerial or business-owner experience.
- **Total interviews:** Nine.
- **Core cases:** Seven retail-SME participants.
- **Comparative cases:** Two interviews in service and wealth-management contexts.
- **Interview format:** Semi-structured, conducted face to face or remotely.
- **Typical duration:** Approximately 20–30 minutes per interview, according to the dissertation methodology.

The published description uses sector-level categories rather than identifiable names or business details.

## Interview topics

The guide explored participants' use of:

- Sales, customer and marketing-performance evidence.
- Managerial experience, tacit knowledge and intuition.
- Decision-making when evidence and judgement disagree.
- Constraints on analytics use in smaller firms.
- AI-supported information and its perceived benefits/risks.

Semi-structured interviewing allowed consistent topics while enabling follow-up questions about specific decisions.

## Thematic analysis workflow

The dissertation followed **Braun and Clarke's six-phase approach**:

1. **Familiarisation:** Read transcripts repeatedly and considered their meaning in context.
2. **Initial coding:** Identified decision-related concepts and coded relevant extracts.
3. **Searching for themes:** Grouped related codes into candidate themes and subthemes.
4. **Reviewing themes:** Compared developing themes with coded material and the wider dataset.
5. **Defining and naming themes:** Refined five final thematic areas.
6. **Reporting findings:** Interpreted themes, compared cases and related insights to existing literature.

## NVivo's role

NVivo supported the organisation of nine transcripts, case setup, coding, retrieval, codebook refinement and cross-case comparison. Framework matrices and analytical memos helped track similarities, differences and developing interpretations.

**NVivo organised the evidence; it did not automatically generate conclusions or make managerial interpretations.**

## Quality and reflexivity

The research discussed credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability. The documented approach included an interview guide, auditability of coding decisions, contextual description, selected evidential quotations within the dissertation and reflexive consideration of the researcher's interpretive role.

## Ethics and data protection

The dissertation reports approval through Newcastle University's ethics process before collection. Participants received information and provided consent; names and identifying business details were anonymised in the academic work.

**The public GitHub portfolio must not contain:**

- Raw/audio recordings or unredacted transcripts.
- Names, contact details or identifiable descriptions of respondents.
- Consent forms or participant information sheets with personal data.
- Private NVivo case files and quotes whose public release was not authorised.

No interview-level dataset is distributed through this repository.

## Study limitations

- Small purposive sample; findings are context-rich, not statistically representative.
- Two comparisons came from non-retail contexts.
- Cross-sectional and self-reported information without corresponding company performance data.
- Interpretation may vary across sectors, business sizes and levels of analytics maturity.

A further study could combine interviews with longitudinal observations or independently measured business performance.
